# What can Coast learn about one viewer’s enjoyment?

The baseline is a private, full-history taste profile per medium. A card compares its title metadata with that profile. The question for each experiment was whether changing the profile, the evidence admitted or the comparison helped the same viewer recognise an unseen favourite or avoid a false high. This register records research decisions. [Product target and acceptance cases](../../taste-match.md)

## Evidence from the copied account

The frozen copy contained 765 movie and 566 show history roots. In the main five-fold replay we hid each test title’s rating, watches, repeats, child feedback and endorsements before fitting. We could score 444 of 444 rated movies and 428 of 429 rated shows. The single-viewer controls disable social inputs and provider aggregate opinion. They still use descriptive title metadata. The earlier ordinal benchmark also has a historical full-feature control in which the provider aggregate can soften personal low ratings. Its dated forecasts use personal ratings alone. Ordering credits a four-star-or-higher title above a three-star-or-lower title from the same test fold, with half credit for a tie. Ranges are the observed minimum and maximum from three deterministic, nested rating samples per fold. They are not uncertainty intervals. All rows use the same hidden targets. *Which* two films happened to be rated changed the result considerably. Ten personal ratings predict more usefully in some combinations than fifty in others.

For the time check, original Trakt dates were joined to local direct ratings by stable provider IDs, medium and rating value. Of 872 direct provider rows, 871 were unique matching Coast roots (444 films and 427 shows). The single repeated mapping agreed on both the exact time and the rating. Data entry came in large batches. A rating day stayed together and only earlier opinions trained the forecast. Present-day title metadata is the remaining approximation. Removing the dates from the model inputs while preserving the training cutoffs changed eight of 183 forecasts in the two fixed windows. All eight were early movies. Ordering in that window went from 76.79% with dates to 79.64% without. The timestamp determined eligibility for the test. Evidence for weighting individual stars by age was poor. ### What “high” actually captured

Across 382 later titles, after the viewer had at least 25 previous direct ratings in that medium, the personal reference was rebuilt using only earlier days. Every future title entered the following counts once.

| Cards with an 80–89 match | Titles | Rated 3½+ | Rated 4+ | Rated 4½+ | Rated 2½ or lower |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Movies | 162 | 153 | 121 | 72 | 5 |
| Shows | 97 | 93 | 76 | 45 | 3 |

Many of the high matches were titles this viewer at least moderately enjoyed. The same band also contains real misses, and it spans several quite different degrees of enjoyment. All 194 scored movies in this chronological test landed at 70 or higher. That is a real limitation of the scale. This viewer chose and rated these titles. The proportions cannot establish the chance of enjoying a random catalogue film, or identify the reason for a particular rating. The literature on self-selection explains why that distinction matters. [Daily forecasts](daily.md) · [Research on self-selected feedback](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v48/schnabel16.html)

### Where the Ghibli exception comes from

We held each unfamiliar film’s other metadata fixed, toggled a studio and a genre or tag in memory, and scored all four combinations with the same private reference. These are tests of the scorer's response to evidence. The modified versions are hypothetical films, with no new ground truth about the viewer.

| Trait pair and comparison set | Neither | First trait alone | Second trait alone | Both |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Ghibli and the anime tag, 83 unseen anime films | 82 | 82 | 31 | 83 |
| Christopher Nolan and science fiction, 566 unseen science-fiction films | 80 | 81 | 81 | 82 |
| Pixar and animation, 622 jointly scorable unseen animated films | 80 | 80 | 82 | 82 |

The cells show median matches using personal ratings and actions. With Coast’s viewing-choice adjustment turned off, the four Ghibli medians became **82, 82, 82 and 83**. With ratings hidden and the adjustment active, they were **61, 61, 15 and 61**. This identifies precisely where a 51-point apparent studio effect comes from. The viewing-choice rule substantially lowers anime and exempts Ghibli. Of the viewer’s rated films with those traits, all three carried both and all three were liked. Five such films were chosen, plus one non-Ghibli anime film with no rating. The data supports explaining the exception as a viewing pattern. The size of the drop is unverified as an enjoyment estimate. For Pixar there were 15 rated animated Pixar films and 45 rated animated films from other studios, providing an actual comparison. ## A personal profile Coast could explain

Keep signed affinities for the viewer’s repeated genres, tags, creators and other attributes. For each claim, retain the number of independent works, the mix of ratings behind it, the type and source of the voluntary actions, and whether the viewer experienced the same trait outside that combination. Explainable examples could identify the closest supporting and contradicting works from that person’s own history. A verified TV creator belongs in this evidence. Sparse evidence and identical tags on very different films should be visible to whoever inspects a false match. A score for a completely unfamiliar, sparsely described title may need to be withheld.

The literature offers several independent reasons to test this design. A content learner can build one reader’s profile from their own examples and rank previously unrated works. Personal case-based retrieval offers a way to represent distinct interests. Film studies distinguish how relevant a tag is to a title from how much the viewer personally likes it. Directly elicited trait preferences can test whether a feature inferred from title ratings really means what we claim. Coast has past title ratings and observations but generally lacks explicit labels explaining *why* someone liked or disliked a title. [Mooney and Roy on learning from one person’s examples](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/9902011) · [Billsus and Pazzani on personal interests](https://ics.uci.edu/~pazzani/Publications/BillsusA.pdf) · [Vig, Sen and Riedl on tag relevance and preference](https://files.grouplens.org/papers/vig-iui2009-tagsplanations.pdf) · [Evaluating inferred film-feature preferences](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.11055)

## Implementation findings

Two primary studies give concrete tests for how Coast records evidence. In a ten-person television study, Kurapati and colleagues compared recommendations derived from viewing diaries, stated preferences and combinations of both. Their combination generally worked best for predicting a participant’s willingness to watch a named show. The authors point out that stated preferences and a stated intention are closely related. Coast can test a harder outcome by saving the prediction before viewing and comparing it with the viewer’s subsequent opinion. In a separate movie study, Costanzo and colleagues found that the model inferred Stan Lee as a preferred actor from Marvel credits even though nobody named him as a favourite. A useful test for a recurring actor is whether the viewer also likes their work outside the franchise where those credits usually appear. [TV study and limitations](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~johnz/pubs/2001_UM.pdf) · [Movie profiling study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.11055)

- The taste history query now counts an imported Jellyfin last-played snapshot at a given instant once. This preserves independent plays and source history. The correction removed 81 excess observations from 11 works on the dev copy. One root changed from repeated to ordinary consumption. The time-aware research harness now uses verified rating times and excludes undated child opinions and current audience totals from its historical training. [History evidence](implicit.md) · [Replay](../../../scripts/taste-replay.ts)
- Fresh whole-catalogue materialisation and per-card evaluation produced identical scores and abstentions across 8,450 roots. A disposable app-level test found a separate stale-cache bug. After an item's genre and credits changed, its warm card stayed at 83% while fresh inspection withheld the match. This demonstrates that target metadata freshness matters even when a personal profile is current. Use the same published profile and current item data for the card and its personal inspection. [Cache experiment and reproduction](materialized.md)

The latter comparison tests the Ghibli finding prospectively. The evaluator requires a later, verified direct rating, deduplicates evidence and compares methods on the same works. No later labels were available at sealing. All three modes use zero peer or provider opinion. ## Reproducing this round

The per-medium capture comes from the existing offline taste replay. Original direct rating dates are a private array of canonical ID, medium, stars and original UTC timestamp. Private account files remain outside the repository. The studies above link their runnable tools. To produce solo-only fold controls, remove the optional audience field from each history observation before passing the capture to the ordinal fold generator. Run the kernel, transfer and calibration experiments against that capture and its saved fold inputs. The no-star and stability checks read only the private capture and original date snapshot. [Prepare folds](ordinal-inputs.ts) · [Scarce and absent ratings](solo.ts)

An earlier external benchmark tested a population-based method before the requirement for a standalone single-viewer model was clarified. Its data and candidate models are excluded from the personal results above. Its runner is retained solely as a separate reproducibility record. [Benchmark and attribution](external-control.md)

The focused database suites passed all ten tests, including the import-date and Jellyfin snapshot cases. The broader checkout still had five failing checks: the backup fixture could not find `pg_dump` on the host path, three tracking projection expectations failed, and an interest-root test put an unexpanded template expression into its SQL fixture. Those checks remain open outside the research measurements. No change was deployed.
